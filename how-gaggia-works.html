    The circuit

    switch it yourself

    Every load in the stock Classic ProEvo Pro, wired like the Classic Pro,E24, wired like the Classic Pro,Classic sits on its own path from line to neutral, and every path runs through a switch or a thermostat. Nothing is computed: if a path is closed end to end, that part runs. The diagram is drawn as a ladder, with line down the left, neutral down the right, and one rung per load.

    Three details surprise people. The thermostats sit on the elements' neutral side, after them. The brew and steam lamps aren't heating lamps: each is wired across its thermostat, so it lights when that thermostat opens, which means ready. And the 184 °C thermal fuse feeds everything except the power lamp, so a blown fuse leaves the power lamp lit and nothing else working.

    Three details surprise people. The power switch breaks the line only, and the thermostats sit on the line side, ahead of the elements. There's one ready lamp, not a heating lamp: it's wired across both thermostats, so it lights whenever heating stops, for brewing or steam. And the 184 °C thermal fuse is in the neutral feed to the whole machine, so a blown fuse leaves everything dark, the power lamp too.

    Try it

    • Turn on Power and let it run: the elements heat until the brew thermostat opens, and the brewready lamp lights each time it does.
    • Turn on Brew: the pump runs, and the 3-way valve opens because its returnfeed through the steam switch is closed.
    • Add Steam: that's hot water mode. The pump keeps running, the valve shuts, and the heater carries on to the steam thermostat.
    • Steam alone: it shorts out the brew thermostat, so heating carries on until the steam thermostat opens at 145 °C and the steamready lamp lights.
    • Drag the temperature past 184 °C: the fuse melts and only the power lamp stays liteverything goes dark until a new one is fitted.

    With Gaggiuino, the brew and steam switches stop switching these rungs and signal the PCB at low voltage instead, and both thermostats leave the elements' circuit: the thermocouple and the SSR take over. With custom wiring that's all the two switches carry; with stock wiring integration, some of their other spades stay on mains. The power switch, the thermal fuse, the pump's protector if yours has one and the earth stay. See what changes.

    Part by part

    13 parts
    1

    Water tank

    A plastic tank of about 2.1 liters, filled through the lid in the cup tray on top or slid out to fill at the sink. A tube from near its floor feeds the pump, and the over-pressure valve's return tube runs back into it.

    How it works

    Nothing clever: the pump sucks water up the intake tube, and the tank isn't pressurized, so anything the over-pressure valve dumps simply runs back in. Keep the end of the intake under water; a pump drawing air rattles and stops pushing water.

    In a Gaggiuino build: unchanged. The optional ToFnLED sits above it, reads the level and lights the tank.

    Vibratory pump

A coil yanks a piston against a spring once per mains cycle, and check valves turn that buzz into one-way flow.

    How it works

    Inside is an iron piston in a tube, with a coil wound around the outside and a spring behind it. A diode in the pump lets only one half of each mains cycle through the coil, so each cycle gives one pulse: the coil pulls the piston against the spring, then the spring pushes it back. Check valves only let water move toward the boiler, so every stroke shoves a little water forward.     3

    Over-pressure valve (OPV)

    A spring-loaded valve between the pump and the boiler, bolted to the group's inlet on your Classic, on top of the pump on the Pro family. When the pressure passes its setting it opens and sends the extra water back to the tank, so the pressure can't climb any further.

    How it works

    A seal is held against a seat by a spring. Water pressure pushes the other way; when it beats the spring, the seal lifts and water escapes down the return tube. The more the pump pushes past the setting, the further it opens, so the line pressure stays close to the setting.

    Boiler

    How it works

    The pump pushes water in through an inlet connector on the group body; hot water leaves for the group through the 3-way valve, and a pipe from the top feeds the steam valve. The brew thermostat screws into its side and the steam thermostat into its top. It's small, so it heats quickly.

    For brewing the brew thermostat holds it a little above 100 °C; the boiler is sealed, so the water stays liquid.     In a Gaggiuino build: kept as it is. The thermocouple screws into the brew thermostat's M4 hole in its side.

    6

    Thermostats and thermal fuse

    A 107 °C brew thermostat screwed into the side of the boiler, a 145 °C steam thermostat in its top, and a 184 °C one-shot thermal fuse. The thermostats switch the elements straight on and off.

    How it works

    Each thermostat holds a bimetal disc that snaps over at its set temperature and pushes a pair of contacts apart; as it cools a few degrees it snaps back and they close. That gap is why the boiler temperature swings. On the Pro family both sit on the elements' neutral side, in series,On your Classic both sit on the line side, ahead of the elements, in series, and the steam switch shorts out the brew thermostat so heating carries on to the steam one.

    The fuse can't reset: once it melts, that path stays open until it's replaced. On the Pro it's in the switched line and feeds everything but the power lamp; on the original Classic it sits in the neutral feed to the whole machine, with the thermostats on the line side instead. It's in the switched line, after the power lamp, so it feeds everything else. On your Classic it sits in the neutral feed to the whole machine, so when it goes everything goes dark, the power lamp too.

    In a Gaggiuino build: both thermostats leave the circuit: the thermocouple goes in the brew thermostat's hole and the SSR does the switching. The fuse stays as the safety cut-out.

    7

    3-way solenoid valve

    An electrically worked valve between the boiler and the group. Powered, it lets boiler water through to the group. Unpowered, it shuts the boiler off and opens the group to a drain tube into the drip tray.

    How it works

    A coil pulls a plunger that seals one port or the other. In the stock circuit it gets power with the brew switch, and its return to neutral runsits feed from the brew switch runs through the steam switch, so it can only open with steam off. That's what makes hot water mode work: brew and steam together run the pump with the valve shut.

    The 230 V Classic sold from 2015 to 2018, the "V2", has no 3-way valve at all.

    In a Gaggiuino build: kept. The PCB opens it from J13, and Gaggiuino depends on it for pressure release and flushing, which is why the V2 can't be converted.

    A stock shot, start to finish

    1. Switch on. The elements heat with the brewready lamp out, and the small boiler warms the brass group under it.
    2. The brew thermostat opens and the brewready lamp lights, but heat already in the elements keeps the water rising for a while: overshoot.
    3. It cools several degrees, the thermostat closes, the lamp goes out, and the cycle repeats for as long as the machine is on. The water temperature at any moment depends on where you are in that cycle.
    4. Owners temperature-surf: flush some water, then time the shot from a lamp change, to land at the same point in the swing every time.
    5. Brew switch on. The pump and the 3-way valve run; pressure builds against the puck, up to the over-pressure valve's setting at most.
    6. Brew switch off. The valve vents the group to the drip tray, so the puck is left drier and the portafilter comes off without spraying.
    7. For milk, switch on steam and wait for the steamready lamp, purge the wand, then steam. Afterward, bring the boiler back down by running water through the group, with no portafilter, before the next shot.

    Gaggiuino replaces steps 2 to 4 with a temperature that holds still, and step 5 with a pressure or flow profile.
