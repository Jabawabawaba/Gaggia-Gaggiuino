Who does what now

before and after

On a stock Gaggia the switches and thermostats do the controlling themselves: each one sits in a mains wire and either completes a load's circuit or breaks it. Gaggiuino moves every decision into one place.

The brew and steam switches stop switching loads and become signals to the PCB. (With stock wiring integration, some of their other spades still carry mains.) A thermocouple and a pressure sensor report what's happening. The PCB reads all of it many times a second and decides what the elements, pump and 3-way valve should do, switching the pump and valve itself and the elements through a solid-state relay. The screen shows it and takes your settings.

What stays: the power switch still breaks mains, the boiler's thermal fuse (184 °C on the official schematics) stays in circuit, so does the pump's thermal fuse, so does the pump's thermal fuse, so does the pump's thermal fuse if yours has one, and earth is bonded exactly as beforeand earth is bonded exactly as beforeand earth is bonded as before, which on a two-pin US Classic means after the grounding retrofitand earth is bonded exactly as beforeand earth is bonded as before, which on a two-pin US Classic means after the grounding retrofitand the earth the grounding retrofit adds stays bonded to the metal. None of those depend on software. With the Shelly, the power switch only signals it: off goes through the Shelly's firmware, and the app or a schedule can switch it back on. Unplug when it has to stay off.

Holding the temperature

thermocouple, PCB, SSR

The thermocouple in the side of the boiler reports the temperature continuously, the PCB decides when the elements should be on, and the SSR switches them. Instead of the stock thermostat's long on and long off, the elements get short pulses as the boiler nears the target, so the temperature settles there instead of swinging around it.

Gen 3's code isn't public, but it uses the same settings as the open Gen 2 firmware. In the Gen 2 code it works in steps. More than 10 °C below the target the elements are simply on. From 10 to 5 °C below they're pulsed for a fixed share of the time, and in the last 5 °C for a smaller share, with the pulses getting shorter as the boiler closes in. At the target they're off. During a shot they run nearly flat out to meet the cold water coming in. That code isn't a PID loop: each step is a fixed rule. Whether Gen 3 changed the details isn't published.
