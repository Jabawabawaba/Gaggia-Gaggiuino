The PCB

in its housing, inside on the right

The control board. Its STM32 microcontroller, an STM32U585 on PCB v4, runs the machine: it reads every sensor and switch, runs the shot profile, and switches the loads. Mains comes in on J10 (neutral) and J11 (line) and feeds a sealed 5 V power supply module on the board. The pump on J12 and the 3-way valve on J13 are switched at mains voltage by solid-state parts on the board, so nothing clicks. The STM32, the sensor chips and the low-voltage headers sit on the other side of an isolation gap, and nothing on the board links the two halves with a wire.

What's on it

  • A MAX31855 chip reads the thermocouple, and an ADS1015 converter reads the pressure sensor's voltage.
  • The pump is switched by a triac, fired through an optocoupler. A zero-cross detector, also behind an optocoupler, tells the STM32 each time the mains wave passes zero, and the STM32 decides cycle by cycle whether to fire it.
  • The 3-way valve is switched by a small optically isolated solid-state relay; a red LED beside it lights whenever the valve is powered.
  • J9 gives the boiler's SSR 5 V on R1 when the element should be on.

New firmware goes on over Wi-Fi from the web page: the screen's, and on PCB v4 the board's own. A board that won't take an update is flashed by wire through its SWD pins (J27) with an ST-Link or WeAct debugger. The USB-C port only powers the board on the bench.

Solid-state relay (SSR)

screwed to the back wall

The switch for the elements. It's an SSR-40DA: a relay controlled by 3 to 32 V DC that switches AC, with no moving contacts. J9 R1 feeds its + input and J9 GND its −. Its two load screws sit in the elements' supply: on stock wiring integration they take the wires that went to the steam thermostat, on two runs of the new harness.

How it works

The input current lights an LED inside the relay. On the other side of an insulating gap, a light-sensitive switch sees it and fires a triac, a semiconductor switch for AC, which carries the elements' current. The PCB and mains only meet through light, which is what keeps the low-voltage side isolated. A zero-cross SSR only turns on where the mains wave crosses zero, and a triac lets go at the next zero, so the elements switch cleanly in whole half-cycles.

While it conducts it drops up to 1.6 V, which at an espresso machine's heater current turns up to about 18 W into heat on a 120 V machine, or about 8 W on a 230 V oneabout 18 W into heat on your 120 V machineabout 8 W into heat on your 230 V machine, so it needs to sit flat on metal. Fork spades on all four screws, as the official schematics draw them, and buy from a known seller: counterfeit SSR-40DAs with undersized triacs are common.

Thermocouple

in the brew thermostat's place, to J4

The temperature sensor: an ungrounded K-type with an M4 thread, from the official parts list. It screws into the boiler where the brew thermostat was, with high-temperature thermal paste and only finger tight.

How it works

Two different alloys, chromel and alumel, are joined at the tip. When the tip is hotter than the far ends, a tiny voltage appears between them, about 41 microvolts per degree. The MAX31855 chip on the PCB measures it, adds the temperature at its own end (the cold junction), and hands the STM32 a reading. "Ungrounded" means the junction is insulated inside the probe's sheath, which matters because the boiler is earthed; see why the low-voltage side floats.

Polarity matters. Swapped wires make the reading fall as the boiler heats, so the controller may keep heating. The public Gen 2 firmware cuts the heater once the reading drops to 0 °C, Gen 3's behavior isn't published, and the boiler's thermal fuse is the last line. That's why the runbook has you watch the temperature rise at first power-on.

Pressure sensor

on a T-fitting or tap block, to J6

A 0 to 1.2 MPa (0 to 12 bar) pressure transducer with a 1/4" thread, powered from 5 V. It's what lets Gaggiuino profile pressure: the PCB reads it continuously and adjusts the pump to follow the curve.

How it works

Water pressure bends a thin metal diaphragm. Strain gauges on the diaphragm change resistance as it flexes, and electronics in the sensor body turn that into a voltage on the signal pin: 0.5 V at 0 bar, rising evenly to 4.5 V at 12 bar. The ADS1015 on the PCB converts that voltage to a number.

On a Gaggia it goes on a T-fitting cut into the line from the pump to the boiler (a push-to-connect T in the Classic's PTFE tube) (a barbed T in the Pro's braided hose, with clamps), or on a pressure tap block clamped between the group and its inlet fittingmetal OPV. Either way it seals on an O-ring, not its thread: the small flat O-ring goes flush into the fitting first, because many fittings are too short for the sensor's own base O-ring to seal, then the sensor goes in hand tight plus at most a quarter turn. On a T-fitting, [UT] Tube Fill pushes the air out of its line.

Screen

on top, to J1

The 4.3" touch screen is a small computer of its own, built on an ESP32-S3 Wi-Fi chip. It draws the interface, stores your profiles and settings, runs the Gaggiuino Wi-Fi network and the web page at gaggiuino.local, and installs updates for itself and the board. It talks to the PCB over a serial link on J1, which also powers it.

It sits on a printed housing with a front, funnel or rear mount. The cable is wired by pin, not by color: blue and yellow swap between cable revisions.

The harness

The official guide gives the Gaggia two ways in. Stock wiring integration keeps the machine's own harness and adds seven short wires: a jumper in place of the brew thermostat, two to the SSR in place of the steam thermostat, and line, neutral, pump and valve wires to the PCB. Custom wiring replaces the harness with new 18 AWG silicone wire, reusing the stock components, following the official schematic for your model and voltage. Switches on low voltage

J7

The brew and steam rockers stay on the front panel, but the PCB now reads them. Each one, when on, connects a PCB input pin to the low-voltage ground: steam to J7 S and brew to J7 B, with the ground back to J7 GND. Brew and steam on together start hot water mode, as on the stock machine.

How it works

A 10 kΩ pull-up resistor on the board holds each input at 3.3 V. So an open switch reads high, which the firmware treats as off. Closing the switch ties the pin to ground and it reads low: on. That's why the power-on checks want under 1 Ω with a switch on and OL with it off.

Mains must never reach these wires: mains on an input pin destroys it, and wiring the pump around the PCB's pump output can short the triac that drives it. On stock wiring integration the guide clears one column of spades on each switch for the low-voltage wires.
